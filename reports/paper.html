Predicting Problematic Internet Use in Youth from Physical, Fitness, and Actigraphy Data: A Gradient-Boosting Study with Rigorous Cross-Validation

Abstract

Problematic internet use (PIU) in children and adolescents is typically assessed with lengthy parent-report questionnaires. We study whether the Severity Impairment Index (SII), a four-level ordinal measure derived from the Parent–Child Internet Addiction Test (PCIAT), can instead be predicted from routinely collected physical, fitness, demographic, and questionnaire measures, together with wrist-worn accelerometer (actigraphy) recordings, using the Healthy Brain Network dataset released for the Child Mind Institute Kaggle competition. Framing SII prediction as ordinal regression with Quadratic-Weighted-Kappa (QWK)-optimized decision thresholds, we build a gradient-boosting pipeline and evaluate every design decision under matched 5-fold × 3-seed out-of-fold (OOF) cross-validation. Our final model — a weight-optimized blend of CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost with CatBoost trained under per-fold pseudo-labeling of unlabeled participants — attains QWK = 0.4623. We report two carefully controlled negative results: (i) wrist actigraphy carries little predictive signal for SII in this cohort (actigraphy-only QWK ≈ 0.19) and does not improve a tabular model, and (ii) the most severe class (1.2% of labels) is effectively unlearnable, with class-weighting providing no benefit under QWK. We emphasize methodological hazards specific to this dataset — target leakage through PCIAT items, a large not-missing-at-random unlabeled subset, and a 20-row public leaderboard — and show how OOF discipline guards against them.

Keywords: problematic internet use, ordinal regression, quadratic weighted kappa, gradient boosting, pseudo-labeling, actigraphy, class imbalance.

1. Introduction

Excessive and compulsive internet use is an increasing concern in pediatric mental health. The standard screening instrument, the Parent–Child Internet Addiction Test (PCIAT) [Young 1998], requires a parent to answer 20 items; the summed score is bucketed into a four-level Severity Impairment Index (SII): none (0), mild (1), moderate (2), severe (3). Because completing the PCIAT is burdensome and depends on an engaged caregiver, a natural question is whether SII can be predicted from measures that are already collected during a clinical or research visit — demographics, physical and fitness assessments, bio-electric impedance, sleep questionnaires — and from objective wearable-sensor data.

This paper reports an end-to-end study on the Healthy Brain Network cohort [Alexander et al. 2017] as distributed by the Child Mind Institute "Problematic Internet Use" competition. Our contributions are:

  1. A reproducible four-phase pipeline (analysis → cleaning → feature engineering → modeling) with all design choices validated by matched out-of-fold cross-validation rather than the public leaderboard.
  2. A careful treatment of three dataset-specific hazards: target leakage (the PCIAT items that define the label are present only in training), label missingness (31% of training rows are unlabeled and the missingness is not at random), and an uninformative 20-row public test.
  3. Two controlled negative results — actigraphy adds no predictive value, and the severe class is unlearnable — each established with a matched baseline, not asserted.
  4. A final model reaching QWK = 0.4623 via pseudo-labeling and OOF-optimized model blending.

2. Related work

Internet-addiction assessment. The PCIAT adapts Young's Internet Addiction Test [Young 1998] to a parent-report format. SII is a monotone bucketing of the PCIAT total, so predicting SII is an ordinal classification problem.

Ordinal targets and QWK. The competition metric is Cohen's quadratic weighted kappa [Cohen 1968], which penalizes predictions by the squared distance from the true ordinal class. A common and effective strategy is to fit a real-valued regressor and then optimize class-boundary thresholds directly for QWK, which we adopt.

Gradient-boosted decision trees. XGBoost [Chen & Guestrin 2016], LightGBM [Ke et al. 2017], and CatBoost [Prokhorenkova et al. 2018] are the standard tools for heterogeneous tabular data with missing values; all three handle NaN natively, which suits this dataset's heavy, structured missingness.

Semi-supervised learning. Pseudo-labeling [Lee 2013] augments training with model-predicted labels for unlabeled data. We use a leakage-safe, per-fold variant with soft (continuous) targets and down-weighting.

Clinical instruments used as features. The FitnessGram battery, the Children's Global Assessment Scale (CGAS) [Shaffer et al. 1983], the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children, and bio-electric impedance analysis (BIA) are all represented in the feature set.

3. Data and exploratory analysis

The dataset contains 3,960 training participants (82 columns) and a 20-row visible test set (59 columns); the scored test set is much larger and hidden. Fields span 11 instruments documented in a data dictionary (81 fields): Demographics, Internet Use, the Children's Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), Physical Measures, FitnessGram (Vitals/Treadmill/Child), bio-electric impedance analysis (BIA), Physical Activity Questionnaires for children and adolescents (PAQ-C/PAQ-A), the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDS), and the PCIAT. Per-participant wrist-accelerometer time series (enmo, anglez, non-wear flag, light, timestamps) are provided at a 5-second cadence for a subset of participants. We devoted a full analysis phase to understanding this structure before any modeling, because — as the figures below show — the dataset's difficulty is driven less by the choice of learner than by its target definition, its missingness, and the weakness of its objective signals.

3.1 Target: derivation, imbalance, and boundary sensitivity

SII is derived deterministically from the PCIAT total score by three cut-points (0–30→0, 31–49→1, 50–79→2, 80+→3); we verified 100% agreement between the recomputed and provided labels. This is a double-edged fact: it confirms the mapping, but it also means the 20 PCIAT-* item columns are the target and must never be used as features (Section 3.4). The class distribution is severely imbalanced, and 416 labeled participants sit within ±2 PCIAT points of a cut-point — near-boundary cases that dominate the achievable QWK.

3.2 Missingness and a biased label set

Only 69.1% of training rows carry a label (1,224 missing SII). Feature missingness is heavy and organized by whole instrument — a participant either completed an instrument or skipped it entirely (PAQ-Adolescent 88%, Fitness-Endurance ~81%, BIA ~50%). We therefore treat instrument completion itself as a feature (per-instrument "was-completed" flags and a per-row missing count).

Crucially, the missing labels are not missing at random. Participants without an SII label differ systematically from labeled ones — they are older, have higher BMI, and report more internet use (Mann–Whitney p ≪ 0.001). Simply discarding unlabeled rows for supervised training therefore introduces a selection bias, which motivates the semi-supervised approach in Section 4.

3.3 Data quality

Exploratory screening surfaced sentinel and physiologically impossible values that would distort any model: CGAS scores of 999 (valid range 1–100), negative BIA body-fat percentages (minimum −8745), extreme BIA masses, and seven implausible BMI rows. These are mapped to missing under documented per-column rules (Section 4).

3.4 What actually predicts SII

The strongest single predictor is the coarsest one: reported computer/internet hours per day (Cramér's V = 0.21). Its relationship with SII is clean and monotone — the share of participants reporting 3+ hours rises from roughly 5% in the None class to nearly half in the Severe class.

Among continuous features, age and body-size measures dominate (Kruskal–Wallis ε² ≈ 0.15). Much of this is an age effect: SII rises with age, and most BIA and fitness measures are themselves age-driven — which is why we add age-normalized features in Section 4 rather than letting the model rediscover age repeatedly.

That mutual correlation is visible directly: the BIA and body-size features form a tightly collinear block, so their individual importances should be read as one shared signal rather than many independent ones.

In short, the exploitable signal is concentrated in a few tabular features — reported internet use, sleep disturbance, physical activity, CGAS, and age-normalized body size — and it is diffuse. This framing sets realistic expectations for the modeling that follows.

4. Methodology

Cleaning. Sentinel and physiologically impossible values are set to missing under documented per-column rules (CGAS ∉ [1,100]; BMI ∉ [8,60]; body-fat ∉ [0,75]; BIA masses/rates ≤ 0; out-of-range vitals, height, weight). No value imputation is performed: the gradient-boosting learners consume NaN directly, and missingness is retained as signal via indicator features.

Feature engineering. From the 58 usable raw columns we derive 77 tabular features (19 engineered): per-instrument "was-completed" flags, a per-row missing count, a merged physical-activity score across the child/adolescent forms, an aggregate FitnessGram-zone score, BIA composition ratios, age-normalized physical measures, and an internet-hours × age exposure term. Categorical/season fields are integer-encoded in a shared category space.

Actigraphy features. Each participant's series is summarized into 31 features: enmo activity statistics and percentiles, wear time and non-wear fraction, days recorded, anglez posture statistics, ambient light, six-hour circadian activity bins, and night/day and weekend/weekday activity ratios.

Modeling. We frame SII as real-valued regression and convert predictions to classes with three thresholds optimized directly for QWK by Nelder–Mead on OOF predictions. Base learners are LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost with mild regularization. All evaluation uses StratifiedKFold (K=5) repeated over three seeds {42, 2024, 7}; reported QWK is the OOF score, and thresholds are fit on OOF predictions to avoid optimistic bias. Test predictions are bagged across fold models.

Pseudo-labeling. To exploit the 1,224 unlabeled rows without leakage, we regenerate pseudo-labels inside every fold: a LightGBM generator trained on the fold's real labels predicts soft (continuous) targets for unlabeled rows, which are appended to that fold's training set with weight 0.3. Validation folds always contain only real labels.

Blending. After selecting each base learner's better pseudo/no-pseudo variant, blend weights over the three models are optimized on OOF QWK (equivalent to linear stacking with a QWK objective).

5. Experiments

We evaluate, each against a matched baseline under identical folds and seeds: (a) tabular-only vs. tabular+actigraphy features; (b) a two-model actigraphy stack, where an actigraphy-only model's OOF prediction becomes a single meta-feature for the tabular model; (c) class-weighting (square-root inverse-frequency) and pseudo-labeling, separately and combined; (d) equal-weight vs. OOF-weight-optimized blending.

Actigraphy stack. An actigraphy-only model reaches just QWK ≈ 0.19 on the 996 covered participants, and folding its OOF prediction into the tabular model as a single meta-feature still underperforms the baseline (0.453 vs. 0.459). Only 36% of labeled rows have actigraphy. The reason is visible directly in the features: mean daytime activity and the night/day activity ratio shift only slightly across SII classes, with heavily overlapping distributions (Figure 9). We conclude that, in this cohort, wrist-movement summaries contain little information about SII.

Class imbalance and pseudo-labeling (Table 2). Class weighting did not improve QWK and never recovered any recall on class 3 (0.00 across all settings; 34 training examples). Per-fold pseudo-labeling improved CatBoost from 0.4591 to 0.4612; it slightly degraded LightGBM/XGBoost, so an equal-weight ensemble did not benefit.

7. Discussion

The predictive signal for SII is concentrated in a handful of tabular features — sleep disturbance, the merged physical-activity score, CGAS, cardiovascular vitals, body size (age-normalized), and reported internet hours — consistent with the univariate analysis. That reported internet hours is the single strongest categorical predictor is intuitive but limited: it is itself a coarse self/parent report, and the remaining signal is diffuse.

The actigraphy null result is the study's most notable finding. A priori, objective movement and circadian-disruption features seemed the most promising route beyond self-report. In practice, actigraphy alone predicts SII barely above chance for this metric (QWK ≈ 0.19), and its low coverage (36%) means the features act mainly as noise for the majority of participants. Simple aggregate statistics may be too coarse; a learned sequence representation could in principle recover more, but the low ceiling implied by the actigraphy-only model tempers expectations.

Pseudo-labeling helped, but selectively — only CatBoost benefited, which is why per-model variant selection before blending mattered. The gains are small (≈ 0.001–0.002 QWK per step) and we are explicit that the final blend's weight optimization on OOF carries a mild overfitting risk; we retain it because the effect is directionally consistent across seeds and because the equal-weight blend of best variants also improved on the original all-equal ensemble.

8. Limitations

  • Ceiling of the label. SII is a coarse bucketing of a self/parent-report instrument; the achievable QWK from indirect measures appears modest.
  • Severe class. With 34 examples, class 3 is effectively unlearnable; because QWK is distance-weighted and this class is 1.2% of labels, the optimizer rationally never predicts it. This bounds recall but costs little QWK.
  • Not-missing-at-random labels. Dropping unlabeled rows for supervised training is not perfectly unbiased; pseudo-labeling mitigates but does not eliminate this.
  • Small public test. With 20 visible rows, all model selection relies on OOF QWK; we report no public or private leaderboard scores.
  • Feature-set ceiling. Remaining ideas (nested threshold optimization, CatBoost hyperparameter search, richer interactions) are each estimated to be worth < 0.005 QWK.

9. Conclusion

Routinely collected physical, fitness, and questionnaire data predict the Severity Impairment Index with QWK ≈ 0.46 under rigorous cross-validation, with reported internet hours, sleep disturbance, activity, and age-normalized body-size the dominant signals. Wrist actigraphy, despite its appeal, contributes almost nothing in this cohort. Careful handling of target leakage, non-random label missingness, and an uninformative public leaderboard — combined with leakage-safe pseudo-labeling and OOF-optimized blending — was more consequential than model choice.
